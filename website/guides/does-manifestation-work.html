Does Manifestation Work? What Psychology Actually Says

9 min read · Manifest Guides · Published: August 2026

Manifestation is the practice of using focused thought and written ritual to bring a desired outcome into your life. Does it work? Split the question in two and psychology answers both halves: several of the daily practices hold up in controlled studies, while the claim that thought alone changes outside events has never passed one. Below is what the research found, including the parts fans skip and the parts skeptics skip.

What would it even mean for manifestation to "work"?

It depends which claim you test. The mystical claim, popularized by Rhonda Byrne's The Secret (2006, over 30 million copies sold), says thoughts carry a frequency and the universe returns what you broadcast. The psychological claim is smaller: a daily ritual of stating a goal and rehearsing it redirects your attention, and redirected attention changes what you attempt.

Science has no instrument for the first claim, and no controlled experiment has ever caught the universe filling an order. The second claim is testable, and researchers have been testing its pieces for decades, mostly without using the word manifestation. The results sort into a pattern: rehearsal helps, fantasy alone hurts, and the belief itself has side effects worth knowing about. Most guides to how to manifest something mention none of the three.

What happened when scientists studied manifestation believers?

In 2023 the question finally got its own measuring stick. Lucas J. Dixon, Matthew J. Hornsey and Nicole Hartley at the University of Queensland built a Manifestation Scale and ran three studies with 1,023 participants, published in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin. One number travels fast: roughly a third of participants endorsed manifestation beliefs. This is not a fringe habit.

The other findings travel slower and matter more. High scorers felt more successful and expected success to come faster. They were also more drawn to risky investments, including cryptocurrency and get-rich-quick schemes, and more likely to have experienced bankruptcy. "Although manifesters felt more confident and optimistic about achieving success, we didn't find objective proof to support the effectiveness of manifestation," Dixon said when the paper came out.

Read that carefully before quoting it at anyone. The study is correlational, so it cannot say manifesting causes bankruptcy; confident optimists may simply be drawn to both the belief and the bets. What it does establish is that the belief keeps expensive company, and that the confidence it produces is not backed by measured results.

Why does imagining success backfire?

Because a vivid fantasy can feel like a down payment. Gabriele Oettingen, professor of psychology at New York University, has studied exactly this for twenty years, and her data is uncomfortable for the vision-board industry. In a 2002 study with Doris Mayer, graduates who spun the most positive fantasies about landing a job had received fewer offers and were earning less two years later than their less dreamy peers. Expectations grounded in past performance predicted success. Free-floating fantasy predicted the opposite.

A follow-up line of research found the mechanism, and its title says it all: "Positive fantasies about idealized futures sap energy". Imagining the achieved outcome relaxes the body, measurably, the way a finished meal does. You get the reward feeling without the work, so the work loses urgency.

Her fix is mental contrasting, not abstinence. In Rethinking Positive Thinking (2014) she argues for "making the most of our fantasies by brushing them up against the very thing most of us are taught to ignore or diminish: the obstacles that stand in our way." Dream it, then drag the obstacle into the frame with it. Good manifestation journaling smuggles this in already. A script that mentions the hard part is mental contrasting wearing incense.

Which parts of the practice does research actually support?

Three pieces of the standard toolkit have direct experimental support once you translate their names, and one piece reliably tests negative. Here is the scorecard with sources.

PracticeWhat the studies foundVerdict
Gratitude journalingIn Emmons & McCullough's 2003 experiments, people keeping weekly gratitude lists for 10 weeks reported more optimism and fewer physical complaints than a group listing hassles.Works
Visualizing the processPham & Taylor, 1999: freshmen who mentally rehearsed studying for a midterm studied more and scored higher. Rehearsing the grade itself did nothing.Works, with a catch
Scheduled repetitionPeter Gollwitzer's implementation intentions, summarized across 94 studies: pre-deciding "when X happens, I do Y" reliably raises follow-through. Fixed morning-noon-night writing slots are this structure.Works
Outcome-only fantasyKappes & Oettingen, 2011: idealized-future fantasies lowered measured energy (systolic blood pressure) and subsequent effort.Backfires

That grid explains something practitioners already feel. A gratitude practice steadies people because it is the component with the cleanest evidence behind it, and scripting feels stronger than daydreaming because it forces process detail into the fantasy. The parts of manifestation that survive the lab are the parts that put a pen in your hand.

When does the practice turn on you?

At the money table, mostly. The Queensland data puts numbers on what financial advisers meet in the wild: once you believe outcomes can be attracted, a crypto bet made on margin stops reading as risk and starts reading as alignment. There is an older, uglier edge too. If thoughts create reality, illness and poverty become evidence of bad thinking, a move The Secret makes almost explicitly, landing the blame on people in circumstances nobody chose. Our lucky girl syndrome guide takes that criticism apart in detail.

My own line, and you are welcome to argue: for habit and relationship goals the mystical frame is mostly harmless and often motivating, but for financial goals it is the single most expensive part of the practice, because it switches off the exact skepticism money decisions need. Keep the ritual. Fire the counterparty.

So how do you practice manifestation honestly?

Run it as an experiment on your own attention, and keep receipts. Here is the version I ran. On March 2 I started a 33-day round of the 369 method for one stalled freelance project, the same present-tense sentence every morning, noon and night. Nothing cosmic happened. Something smaller did: by day 19 the sentence had annoyed me into emailing three dormant contacts I had been avoiding since November, one of them answered, and the contract signed in April had my fingerprints on every step of it. Eighteen repetitions a day make a goal impossible to ignore. That is the whole trick, and it is a good trick.

  1. Pick one goal and write it as one specific sentence.
  2. Name the obstacle most likely to stop you, on paper, right next to it. That is Oettingen's contrast, and it is the step every influencer version deletes.
  3. Do one thing per day that only makes sense if the sentence is coming true.
  4. Log what happens in a manifestation journal, misses included. An evidence log you can't argue with beats a feeling you can.

If the log fills up, keep going. If it stays empty for a month, change the goal or change the method. The practice should answer to your data like everything else does.

FAQ

Is there scientific evidence that manifestation works?

There is evidence for its components and none for its cosmology. Gratitude journaling and if-then planning improve measured outcomes in controlled studies, and visualization helps when you rehearse the work rather than the reward. No experiment has shown thought alone changing external events.

What did the 2023 manifestation study actually find?

Dixon and colleagues found that about one in three people endorse manifestation beliefs, and that believers felt more successful while also being more drawn to risky investments and more likely to have experienced bankruptcy. The study is correlational: it maps the company the belief keeps and proves no direction of cause.

Is manifestation just a placebo?

Placebo is the wrong frame, because there is a real active ingredient: structured attention. Writing one goal eighteen times a day changes what you notice and attempt, which changes results through ordinary causation. Where belief alone does the lifting, the job-search data suggests the lift can even be negative.

Should I stop practicing manifestation?

Keep the ritual if it keeps you acting, because the routines are where the documented benefits live. The one part worth dropping today is letting the belief near your investment decisions. If a financial choice only makes sense with the universe as a counterparty, it does not make sense.
