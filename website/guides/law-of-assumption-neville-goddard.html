Law of Assumption: How to Practice It (SATS Method)

7 min read · Manifest Guides · Updated: November 2026

The law of assumption is the idea that whatever you assume to be true, and feel as true, will eventually show up in your life. It is usually traced to the writer Neville Goddard (1905–1972), and it differs from the general law of attraction by giving you a specific exercise: mentally occupy the wish as already fulfilled. Nothing in the research shows it changes outside events, but as a rehearsal tool for confidence and action it has a sensible core. Here is how it works, the five-step practice, and where the evidence stops.

Quick answer

Practice: (1) pick one specific wish, (2) write a one-sentence assumption in the present tense, (3) build a 30-second scene that could only happen if the wish were true, (4) replay that scene nightly as you fall asleep (SATS, "State Akin to Sleep"), and (5) act in line with the assumption during the day. Run it for about a month, then review what you did, not only what happened.

What is the law of assumption?

It is the claim that imagination plus feeling creates reality: you assume a desire is already yours, hold that assumption, and your circumstances are said to rearrange to match. Neville Goddard taught a version of this in lectures and books such as Feeling Is the Secret (1944) and The Power of Awareness (1952). The phrase "law of assumption" itself became a popular label much later, largely through social media, and today's explainers are mostly interpretations of his work rather than his own words.

That distinction matters for expectations. The practice is a belief system with techniques attached, not a tested method. The sections below treat it that way: useful where it overlaps with ordinary psychology, unproven where it claims to bend events.

What are SATS, revision, and the mental diet?

  • SATS (State Akin to Sleep): as you drift off, replay one short scene that implies the wish is fulfilled, so the feeling is the last thing in your mind before sleep.
  • Revision: mentally replay a past event with the ending you wish had happened, instead of the one that did. It is a way of reframing memory, not of denying the facts.
  • Mental diet: notice recurring self-talk during the day ("I never get picked") and deliberately swap it for the assumed version.
  • Persistence: when doubt appears, return to the assumption rather than debating it.

How do you practice the law of assumption? (5 steps)

1. Pick one specific wish

"A job I enjoy" is vague. "A product design role with a team that ships weekly" gives your scene something concrete to contain. Start with something realistic; a first attempt at a huge goal mostly teaches you to doubt the method.

2. Write the assumption in one present-tense sentence

"I work as a product designer and I like my Mondays." Writing it by hand or in a manifestation journal forces you to notice if a word feels false, which is your cue to reword it until it is believable.

3. Build a 30-second scene

Choose a moment that only exists if the wish came true: a colleague asking about your design file, your badge scanning at the door. Include one sensory detail and one feeling. The scene, not the wish, is what you rehearse. The scripting method uses the same idea on paper.

4. Replay it nightly as you fall asleep

Lie down, run the scene slowly from the inside (what you see, hear, feel), and let sleep take over. If you fall asleep mid-scene, that is the point of SATS. Most guides suggest 21 to 30 nights; treat that as a trial length, not a promise.

5. Act in line with the assumption

This step is the one popular explainers play down, and the research below says it is the one that matters. If you assume you are a designer, update the portfolio, send the application, ask for the introduction.

Does the law of assumption actually work?

No study shows that assuming a wish is fulfilled changes what happens outside your head. There is, however, a relevant finding going the other way. In experiments by Heather Barry Kappes and Gabriele Oettingen (Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 2011), people who indulged in positive fantasies about a desired future showed less energy and effort toward it than people who did not. Oettingen's later work found that "mental contrasting", picturing the wish and then the real obstacle, with an if-then plan, tends to improve follow-through.

The sensible reading: rehearsing the feeling of success can steady nerves before an interview or a hard conversation, but only if it ends in a plan and an action. Pure fantasy at bedtime, with nothing done during the day, is the version most likely to leave you feeling worse in a month. For the wider evidence, see does manifestation work?, and if you have been at it a while without movement, why manifestation is not working covers the usual reasons.

Common mistakes

  • Treating doubt as failure. Doubt arrives for everyone. The practice is to return to the scene, not to feel certain.
  • Picking an outcome you do not control. "My ex texts me" depends on someone else. "I feel settled and open to dating" is something you can actually assume and act on.
  • Skipping the daytime step. The scene without action is rehearsal with no performance.
  • Switching wishes weekly. One wish per month gives the scene enough repetition to mean something.

FAQ

What is the difference between the law of assumption and the law of attraction?

The law of attraction says your thoughts and feelings draw matching events toward you. The law of assumption makes a narrower claim: that you should deliberately assume the wish is already fulfilled and live from that assumption. In practice the first is passive ("keep a positive vibe") and the second is an active mental exercise, which is why it comes with named techniques like SATS and revision.

Does the law of assumption work?

There is no scientific evidence that assuming a wish is fulfilled changes external events. There is evidence that vividly fantasizing about a positive outcome can lower effort toward it, while pairing a wish with an honest look at the obstacles and a plan tends to help. Used as a way to rehearse confidence and then act, it is reasonable; used as a replacement for action, it can backfire.

What does SATS stand for?

SATS stands for State Akin to Sleep. It is the practice of lying in bed as you drift off and mentally replaying one short scene that implies your wish has come true, so the feeling of it is the last thing on your mind before sleep. It is drawn from Neville Goddard's teaching that the drowsy state is when imagination is most absorbing.
